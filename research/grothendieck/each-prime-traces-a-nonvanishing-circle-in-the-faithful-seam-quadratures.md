# Each prime traces a nonvanishing circle in the faithful seam quadratures

## Explicit quadratures

Let

\[
a=p^{-1/2},
\qquad
u=z\log p.
\]

For

\[
D_+=1-ae^{-u},
\qquad
D_-=1-ae^u,
\]

the faithful even and odd quadratures are

\[
T=D_++D_-=2(1-a\cosh u),
\]

\[
A=D_+-D_-=2a\sinh u.
\]

## The seam circle

On the critical seam \(z=it\), write \(\theta=t\log p\) and

\[
B=-iA=2a\sin\theta.
\]

Then \(T\) and \(B\) are real and satisfy

\[
(T-2)^2+B^2=4a^2=\frac4p.
\]

Thus the local faithful comparison traces a circle centered at \((2,0)\)
with radius \(2/\sqrt p\).

## Nonvanishing

For every prime \(p\),

\[
\frac2{\sqrt p}<2.
\]

The circle does not contain or enclose the origin. In particular,

\[
T\geq2\left(1-\frac1{\sqrt p}\right)>0.
\]

The local two-quadrature relationship therefore never becomes scalar-null on
the seam and has zero winding around the origin.

## Meaning of the circle

The circle is not a zero locus. It is the orbit of the local Tate amplitude
pair under reciprocal unitary phase. Its center is the common identity
component; its radius is the prime-weighted interaction amplitude.

This supplies a precise answer to the earlier geometric intuition about oval
or circular spectral traces. After using the source-native quadratures and
the seam metric, the orbit is exactly circular. Distorted scalar plots arise
from projecting or rescaling these two channels unequally.

## Global implication and limit

No individual prime can create a scalar-null relationship. Any zero in a
completed global scalar section must arise from coupling, infinite
completion, or projection across primes and the archimedean channel.

This is not a proof of RH. Products or sums of individually nonvanishing
local comparisons may acquire global zeros, and the restricted product is
not an ordinary product of these circles.

## Result

Every prime determines an exact, nonvanishing seam circle in the faithful
even–odd quadrature plane. The circle records the local relationship rather
than a local zero. Global zero formation is necessarily a higher-level
coupling or completion phenomenon.
