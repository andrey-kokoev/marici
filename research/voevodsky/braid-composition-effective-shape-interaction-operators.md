## 1. Research objective

Construct a universal interaction law capable of producing three observed outcome classes:

1. Two original objects survive.
2. Two original objects transform into two output objects.
3. Two original objects transform into three output objects.

Reference processes:

e⁻ + e⁺ → e⁻ + e⁺
e⁻ + e⁺ → 2γ
e⁻ + e⁺ → 3γ

Adopt the working frequency constraint:

P(2γ) / P(3γ) = 372

Use observed reactions and correlations as constraints. Do not assume conventional subatomic particle models as the underlying ontology.

The mathematical research proceeded through:

- Braid-group representations
- Configuration spaces of interacting shapes
- Geometric encounter measures
- Triangulation-independent composition
- Two-dimensional cobordisms
- Frobenius algebras
- Coherent transition operators
- Elimination of internal interaction states

The final construction is an energy-dependent effective operator on three observable channels.

## 2. Corrected braid-group classification

Let B₃ be the three-strand braid group.

Its endpoint-permutation homomorphism is:

π: B₃ → S₃

Require strand 1 to return to its original attachment.

Define:

G = {b ∈ B₃ : π(b)(1) = 1}

The permitted endpoint permutations are:

π(G) = {e, (23)}

Therefore:

G/P₃ ≅ S₂

where P₃ is the pure three-strand braid group.

The standard closure of a braid has as many connected components as the number of cycles in its endpoint permutation.

| Permutation | Components | Candidate channel |
|---|---:|---|
| Identity | 3 | 2→3 |
| (23) | 2 | 2→2 |

The two classes are:

G₃ = P₃

G₂ = σ₂P₃

Each contains infinitely many braid histories.

Only G₃ is a subgroup. G₂ is a coset.

This establishes a classification of closure-component counts, rather than a classification of individual braid histories or physical scattering amplitudes.

## 3. Canonical channel representation

Introduce a two-dimensional state space:

Hc = span{|3⟩, |2⟩}

The endpoint-permutation representation is:

ρc(g) = I, if g ∈ P₃

ρc(g) = X, if g ∈ σ₂P₃

where:

X = [[0,1],[1,0]]

This representation respects composition:

ρc(gh) = ρc(g)ρc(h)

It records which closure-component channel a braid occupies.

However, all histories within the same coset have identical representations.

Thus endpoint-permutation information alone cannot determine interference between different histories.

## 4. Retaining braid-history information

Introduce a two-dimensional internal state space.

Use the unitary matrices:

A = (I + iσz)/√2

B = (I + iσx)/√2

They satisfy:

ABA = BAB

AB ≠ BA

Therefore they define a noncommutative unitary representation of B₃.

A combined representation can be constructed:

ρ(g) = X^ε(g) ⊗ U(g)

where:

ε(g) = 0 for g ∈ P₃

ε(g) = 1 for g ∈ σ₂P₃

Consider:

g₁ = σ₂

g₂ = σ₁²σ₂

Both belong to the two-component channel.

For the internal initial state |0⟩:

U(g₁)|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2

U(g₂)|0⟩ = (i|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2

These states are orthogonal.

Result:

Distinct braid histories can produce the same output multiplicity while retaining distinguishable internal states.

The representation preserves composition but does not specify the probability of choosing a particular history.

## 5. Symmetric stochastic history model

Test an equal-probability random walk on braid generators:

σ₁, σ₁⁻¹, σ₂, σ₂⁻¹

Use a Poisson-distributed number of elementary crossings, with mean t.

Project the walk into S₃.

The resulting six-state random walk is equivalent to a continuous-time walk on a six-cycle.

The endpoint probabilities are:

P(e) =
[1 + 2exp(−t/2) + 2exp(−3t/2) + exp(−2t)]/6

P((23)) =
[1 + exp(−t/2) − exp(−3t/2) − exp(−2t)]/6

Conditioning on preservation of strand 1, the channel probability ratio is:

R(t) = P((23)) / P(e)

For positive finite t:

R(t) < 1

At long times:

R(t) → 1

This stochastic model favors the three-component channel or approaches equal frequencies.

It does not produce the working ratio 372:1.

## 6. Coherent sum over braid histories

Replace stochastic composition with unitary evolution.

Within the subgroup generated by:

a = σ₂

b = σ₁²

define the Hermitian operator:

H = (a + a⁻¹ + b + b⁻¹)/4

The braid elements act by left multiplication on the history-state space.

Define:

U(t) = exp(−itH)

Starting from the identity braid, expansion of U(t) coherently sums sequences of elementary operations.

Different sequences representing the same braid contribute to the same state amplitude.

At small t:

P(2) = t²/8 + O(t⁴)

P(3) = 1 − t²/8 + O(t⁴)

This constructs reversible coherent history dynamics.

The result depends on the selected generator set, the equal generator weights, and the evolution parameter.

The generator pair a,b defines a subgroup of G; it was not established that this pair generates all of G.

## 7. Crossing-phase representation

A further representation retains signed crossing number.

Let:

w(g) = signed crossing number

ε(g) = endpoint-permutation parity

Define:

ρφ(g) = exp(iφw(g)) X^ε(g)

The phase parameter φ changes interference without changing the endpoint-permutation class.

Applying this representation to the preceding interaction generator gives:

Hφ = (cos φ/2)X + (cos 2φ/2)I

The channel probabilities become:

P(2) = sin²(t cos φ/2)

P(3) = cos²(t cos φ/2)

Thus the same endpoint classification permits different output probabilities.

The phase φ remains undetermined.

## 8. Dimensional dependence of exchange topology

The permitted exchange histories depend on the dimension and constraints of the configuration space.

For freely moving indistinguishable points:

| Configuration dimension | Exchange group |
|---|---|
| 2 | Braid group |
| 3 or greater | Permutation group |

For scalar representations of free exchanges in three dimensions:

exp(iφ) ∈ {+1,−1}

The continuously adjustable scalar braid phase is therefore unavailable when the configuration space permits unrestricted three-dimensional exchanges.

This conclusion does not apply automatically to extended, tethered, or topologically constrained objects.

The relevant exchange group must be calculated from the actual shape-configuration space.

## 9. Configuration space of two tetrahedra

Return to two congruent rigid tetrahedra sharing an edge.

Fix the first tetrahedron.

The second can rotate around the shared edge.

For regular tetrahedra, the internal dihedral angle is:

α = arccos(1/3) ≈ 70.529°

Requiring non-overlapping interiors gives the relative angular interval:

α ≤ θ ≤ 2π − α

Its width is:

L = 2π − 2α ≈ 218.942°

The interior configurations share an edge.

At either endpoint, additional vertex alignment produces face contact.

The collision-free configuration space is an interval.

Therefore:

π₁(Cedge) = 0

There is no nontrivial winding group for this particular rigid, edge-sharing configuration.

Result:

The earlier braid topology is not intrinsic to this restricted tetrahedral geometry.

It would require a different configuration space or additional permitted motions.

## 10. Boundary encounters and interaction probability

Represent relative angular configuration by:

0 < x < L

Let:

D = angular diffusion coefficient

λ = interior termination rate

Define q(x) as the probability of reaching a face-contact boundary before interior termination.

It satisfies:

Dq''(x) − λq(x) = 0

q(0) = q(L) = 1

Writing κ = √(λ/D):

q(x) =
cosh[κ(x−L/2)] / cosh(κL/2)

For uniformly distributed initial angles:

P(face) = 2 tanh(κL/2)/(κL)

Assigning face contact to three-output recombination and interior termination to two-output recombination gives a two-channel probability model.

The working ratio 372:1 requires approximately:

κL = 746

The model therefore constrains the ratio between angular exploration and interior termination.

It does not derive that ratio from simplex geometry.

## 11. Competing geometric transition rules

A second model assigns two different geometric termination conditions.

For two tetrahedra sharing an edge:

- Face-contact boundaries select three outputs.
- A distinguished interior angular configuration selects two outputs.

Choosing maximum angular separation as the interior condition gives:

θ = π

Under unbiased diffusion and uniformly distributed initial configurations, the two outcomes have equal probability:

P(2) = P(3) = 1/2

The ratio is 1:1.

The selection of θ = π as a recombination condition was not derived from incidence.

## 12. Minimum-length reconnection

Another candidate selects whichever local reconnection minimizes total connection length.

For four endpoints a,b,c,d:

E₂ = |a−c| + |b−d|

E₃ = |a−b| + |c−d|

Choose the smaller value.

For a rectangular endpoint configuration:

E₂ = 4h

E₃ = 4w

This selects different outputs according to the aspect ratio.

A symmetric distribution under exchange of h and w gives equal frequencies.

Introducing a distinguished axis produces unequal frequencies, but the tested isotropic model gave:

P(2)/P(3) = √2 − 1

This favors three outputs.

Using the full regular-tetrahedron geometry and the specified reconnection lengths gave:

P(2) ≈ 0.177866

P(3) ≈ 0.822134

P(2)/P(3) ≈ 0.216347

The minimum-length criterion also selected the wrong channel at face contact.

Result:

Minimum-length reconnection is inconsistent with the proposed incidence-to-multiplicity assignment.

## 13. Configuration-space codimension

Generalize from edge-sharing tetrahedra to freely positioned rigid tetrahedra.

Their relative configuration space has six degrees of freedom:

- Three translational
- Three rotational

Under the chosen exact-alignment model:

| Contact condition | Free dimensions | Codimension |
|---|---:|---:|
| Unrestricted | 6 | 0 |
| Shared edge | 1 | 5 |
| Shared face | 0 | 6 |

Introduce a small geometric tolerance ε.

The corresponding neighborhood measures have leading scaling:

P(edge) ~ Ce ε⁵

P(face) ~ Cf ε⁶

Therefore:

P(edge)/P(face) ~ (Ce/Cf)/ε

The inverse-resolution exponent follows from the one-unit codimension difference.

The coefficients depend on the measure and geometric definition of proximity.

## 14. A normalized coordinate model

Normalize the six local configuration coordinates to unit ranges.

Require five coordinates to satisfy the edge-alignment tolerance.

Face contact requires an additional coordinate to approach one of two boundary positions.

Under independent uniform-coordinate assumptions:

P(edge) = ε⁵

P(face) = 2ε⁶

Classify edge-only encounters by subtracting face encounters:

P(edge-only) = ε⁵ − 2ε⁶

Thus:

R = P(edge-only)/P(face)

R = 1/(2ε) − 1

For R = 372:

ε = 1/746

This identifies the resolution required by the observed frequency ratio under the declared measure.

The numerical coefficient is measure-dependent. The calculation does not establish a universal geometric resolution.

## 16. Coherence under subdivision

Let sd denote barycentric subdivision and μ the proposed recombination.

Test:

sd ∘ μ ≅ μ ∘ sd

The first composition is defined: recombine, then subdivide.

The second was undefined under the original local rule because subdivision removes the original pair of maximal tetrahedra as recognizable cells.

Parent-cell data can be retained to extend μ and enforce compatibility.

But this makes coherence dependent on retained representation information.

The issue is structural:

The proposed recombination law acts on a chosen triangulation rather than intrinsically on the underlying shape.

## 17. Triangulation-independent shape interactions

Treat a shape as an equivalence class of piecewise-linear triangulations.

Pachner moves relate triangulations of the same piecewise-linear manifold.

An intrinsic interaction should be compatible with replacing either input triangulation by an equivalent one.

Our original transformation:

2 tetrahedra → 3 triangles

changes the dimension of the represented complex.

The initial complex is a three-dimensional ball.

The proposed output is a two-dimensional disk.

These are not equivalent triangulations of the same shape.

Therefore, the transformation should be represented as a correspondence between different shapes, rather than an equivalence between triangulations.

Introduce an interaction object W connecting input and output structures.

The interaction becomes a morphism or correspondence.

Triangulations represent W but do not define the identity of the interaction.

## 18. Two-dimensional cobordism construction

Use oriented two-dimensional cobordisms as the first explicit triangulation-independent model.

Represent each incoming or outgoing component by a circle.

Let W connect two incoming circles to k outgoing circles.

For a connected orientable surface of genus g:

χ(W) = 2 − 2g − b

where b is the number of boundary circles.

For two incoming and k outgoing components:

b = 2 + k

Hence:

χ(W) = −2g − k

For genus zero:

| Interaction | Boundary circles | Euler characteristic |
|---|---:|---:|
| 2→2 | 4 | −2 |
| 2→3 | 5 | −3 |

The two channels correspond to distinct interaction surfaces.

Their classifications are independent of a particular triangulation.

## 19. Universal merge and split operations

Introduce two elementary cobordisms.

Merge:

m: 2 → 1

Split:

Δ: 1 → 2

The two-output surface is:

W₂ = Δ ∘ m

The three-output surface is:

W₃ = (Δ ⊔ id) ∘ Δ ∘ m

Thus both outcomes are generated by the same elementary operations.

The three-output channel requires one additional split.

The cobordism category permits reversing the direction of a surface by interchanging its input and output boundaries.

This reversal is not generally a two-sided categorical inverse.

The construction establishes a common compositional grammar for different output multiplicities.

## 20. Frobenius algebra representation

An oriented two-dimensional topological quantum field theory assigns a commutative Frobenius algebra A to the circle.

The elementary surfaces become linear operators:

m: A⊗A → A

Δ: A → A⊗A

The two interaction channels become:

M₂ = Δm

M₃ = (Δ⊗I)Δm

The Frobenius compatibility condition is:

(m⊗I)(I⊗Δ) = Δm = (I⊗m)(Δ⊗I)

This supplies algebraic coherence between alternative surface decompositions.

## 21. Explicit two-state Frobenius algebra

Choose:

A = ℂ²

with basis e₀,e₁.

Define:

m(eᵢ⊗eⱼ) = δᵢⱼ eᵢ

| Input | M₂ | M₃ |

The algebra determines which internal labels can compose.

It does not determine how frequently M₂ or M₃ occurs.

## 22. Unified observable composition operator

Let P project onto matching input labels.

For the chosen orthonormal basis:

M₂†M₂ = P

M₃†M₃ = P

Introduce:

q = total conversion probability

p = probability of two outputs conditional on conversion

Define the combined operator:

Sψ = (
  (I−P+√(1−q)P)ψ,
  √(qp) M₂ψ,
  √(q(1−p)) M₃ψ
)

The outputs occupy three mutually orthogonal channel spaces.

Direct calculation gives:

S†S = I

Thus S is an isometry.

It preserves total probability and can be extended to a unitary operator on a sufficiently large space.

For matching input labels:

| Outcome | Probability |
|---|---|
| Original objects survive | 1−q |
| Two output objects | qp |
| Three output objects | q(1−p) |

The working ratio gives:

p/(1−p) = 372

p = 372/373

The algebra determines the maps but leaves p and q free.

## 23. Equal-coupling three-channel model

Test whether additional structural restrictions determine the channel probabilities.

Introduce three states:

|E⟩ = original pair survives

|2⟩ = two output objects

|3⟩ = three output objects

Assume successive transitions:

  [1,0,1],
  [0,1,0]

Define:

P(3) = (1−cos x)²/4

P(2)² = 4P(E)P(3)

This is a parameter-free relation within the equal-coupling model.

For:

P(2)/P(3) = 372

P(E):P(2):P(3) = 34596:372:1

| Outcome | Probability |

These are predictions of the assumed equal-coupling model, not measured branching fractions.

## 24. Symmetric-square interpretation

The preceding three-state system is the symmetric square of a binary operation.

Let X exchange two binary basis states.

P(E) = (1−s)²

P(E):P(2):P(3) = 186²:2(186):1

This explains the numerical structure of the equal-coupling model.

The central issue is whether the binary factors correspond to actual elementary shape transformations.

## 25. Topological test of elementary operations

A local strand reconnection changes the number of closed components by one.

But the proposed state sequence begins:

Original pair → two output objects

Both sides contain two components.

Therefore this transition cannot be represented by a single ordinary reconnection that changes component number by one.

The symmetric-square model does not directly implement the topology of the proposed interaction.

Use the merge-and-split structure instead:

2 original components
→ 1 connected intermediate
→ 2 output components
→ 3 output components

This gives a sequence of three elementary shape operations.

The connected intermediate must be retained in the construction.

## 26. Four-state interaction model

Introduce four states:

|E⟩ = original pair

|C⟩ = connected intermediate

|2⟩ = two output objects

|3⟩ = three output objects

Assign coupling strengths:

a = merge coupling

b = first split coupling

c = additional split coupling

Define:

H = [
  [0,a,0,0],
  [a,0,b,0],
  [0,b,0,c],
  [0,0,c,0]
]

The operator is Hermitian for real a,b,c.

Its evolution is unitary:

U(t) = exp(−itH)

The interaction follows:

E ↔ C ↔ 2 ↔ 3

This distinguishes shape-identity conversion from changes in component count.

## 27. Short-interaction expansion

Start in |E⟩.

At small t, the first nonzero amplitude for |2⟩ requires two elementary transitions.

The first nonzero amplitude for |3⟩ requires three.

Consequently:

P(2) = a²b²t⁴/4 + O(t⁶)

P(3) = a²b²c²t⁶/36 + O(t⁸)

Their leading ratio is:

P(2)/P(3) ~ 9/(c²t²)

The merge coupling a and first split coupling b cancel.

The relative multiplicity probability is controlled, to leading order, by the additional split.

For the working ratio:

c t ≈ 3/√372 ≈ 0.15554

This is a model constraint, not an independently determined coupling.

The earlier three-channel binomial relation is not generally preserved.

## 28. Eliminating the connected intermediate

The intermediate |C⟩ is required for constructive composition.

However, the observable channel set is:

Hobs = span{|E⟩,|2⟩,|3⟩}

Separate the full Hamiltonian into observable and intermediate blocks.

Use the resolvent:

G(z) = (zI−H)⁻¹

Eliminate |C⟩ through the Schur complement.

The resulting energy-dependent effective operator is:

H_eff(z) = [
  [a²/z, ab/z, 0],
  [ab/z, b²/z, c],
  [0, c, 0]
]

Here z is the complex spectral parameter, with z ≠ 0 in this representation.

This is an exact elimination of the internal state at the resolvent level.

## 29. Interpretation of the effective operator

The effective operator has three structural features.

### Effective conversion

The merge and first split combine into an effective transition:

E ↔ 2

with coupling:

ab/z

### Additional splitting

The transition:

2 ↔ 3

retains coupling:

c

### Spectral dependence

Eliminating the connected intermediate introduces terms proportional to 1/z.

This is the spectral representation of nonlocal effective dynamics after eliminating an internal degree of freedom.

The intermediate state remains dynamically relevant even when it is not included as an explicit observable channel.

## 30. Exact resolvent relation

Let:

G_2E(z) = ⟨2|(zI−H)⁻¹|E⟩

G_3E(z) = ⟨3|(zI−H)⁻¹|E⟩

The final row of the resolvent equation gives:

z G_3E(z) = c G_2E(z)

Therefore:

G_3E(z) = (c/z)G_2E(z)

Taking squared magnitudes:

|G_2E(z)|² / |G_3E(z)|² = |z|²/c²

This result is independent of a and b.

It follows exactly from the four-state Hamiltonian.

Green-function magnitudes are not automatically observable scattering probabilities. A scattering construction also requires asymptotic states, channel normalization, and an appropriate relation between the resolvent and the scattering operator.
