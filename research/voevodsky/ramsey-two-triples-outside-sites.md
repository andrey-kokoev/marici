# What a single obstructed triple forces before any coverage assumption

Assume a minimum pairing repair, with single rows and pairs legal in blue. Each obstructed triple contains a matched pair of disjoint rows. At most one square port can join the triple: the adjacent-corner and opposite-corner arguments in `ramsey-three-port-missed-site-proof.md` use only disjointness and pair legality, not outside coverage. Also the fixed site and a square port cannot join each other. Hence at least three of the four old vertices must be nonfixed sites. The disjoint rows use six of the nine nonfixed sites. All three remaining sites are therefore in the witness, mutually blue. The third row lies entirely in the union of the matched pair.

Each matched row uses one or two movable sites. Two double-movable rows leave three outside sites, which cannot all be blue. Two single-movable rows on opposite axes both miss a square corner, forbidding their repair link. On the same axis their disjointness forces opposite sites, leaving the adjacent sites of the other axis among the three supposed blue vertices. Thus each matched pair has one double-movable row and one single-movable row. Its six sites include three outside sites; the witness uses the other two.

The third row is single-movable, on the same axis as the single-movable matched row, by the axis test in `ramsey-repair-hinge-uniqueness.md`. Briefly, if the matched rows use `(x0,y0)` and `x1`, the three compulsory blue sites include `y1`. The third row misses `y1`; if it met `y0`, the two square ports through `y0` would produce a forbidden seven with the third and double-movable rows alone.

For two triples requiring different members of the matching, each thus contains only its own matched double-movable row. The two shared rows are single-movable. If their outside pairs differ, their union already covers all three outside sites of either triple. Hence the two missed outside sites coincide. If the pairs agree, the preceding argument alone fixes only two of the three covered sites. The two shared rows must use opposite sites on the same movable axis (otherwise they are identical). The census has 192 cases of different outside pairs and 84 of equal pairs.

**Equal outside pairs: the common-neighbor contradiction.** Denote the two single-movable rows by P0 and P1. They use x0 and x1 respectively and the same outside pair S. Each double-movable row must be disjoint from the single row to which it is matched. Thus we can label the doubles D0,D1 so that Di uses xi and is matched to P(1−i). Suppose their outside sites a (for D0) and b (for D1) differ. Neither belongs to S. Let h be the fifth outside site. In the obstructed triple with D0 the two missed sites b,h are mutually blue. In the triple with D1 the missed sites a,h are mutually blue.

Consider the blue pair P0,D0: they share x0, so their mutual link cannot be red. Both square ports through x0 are blue to this pair, to x1, and to the outside sites b,h. The sites x1,b,h are also missed by both rows. To avoid a blue seven, x1 must be red to b or h. But x1 and b lie together in D1, so x1–b must be blue. Thus x1–h is red. Applying the same argument to P1,D1 forces x0–h red: x0 cannot be red to a inside D0. Red links from a movable site to an outside site are physical path edges, not pentagon edges. The consecutive sites x0,x1 have no common path neighbor h. This contradiction proves a=b. Both triples therefore cover S plus the same third outside site in the equal-pair case too.

This argument forces the shared missed edge for the two triples that require distinct matching links, with no census assumption on their outside coverage. Each matched pair covers all three outside sites of its obstructed triple, since the third row lies in the union of that pair. The two matched pairs therefore cover the same three outside sites. Deleting any one row leaves the other matched pair intact, so every triple misses the same two sites.
