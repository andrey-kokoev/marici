# Why three-corner repairs change at width ten

Assume the three distinct corner rows of a literal square, two new rows S,T legal individually, mutually blue ports initially, and the red-path chart with triangle/seven bans. Write the elbow as C={f,x,y}, the tips as D={f,X,y}, E={f,x,Y}. Capitals X,Y here denote alternative sites, not colors. The missing fourth corner {f,X,Y} is a separated triple. Write U=C∪D∪E. A corner is removable if it is disjoint from S or T: a red link then prevents it from occurring in a blue seven containing both newcomers. If S,T are disjoint, their single red link already suffices; assume they overlap. Every obstruction uses both newcomers. Red links from different parents to newcomers are mutually compatible: all parent-parent links and the S–T link stay blue.

## Why all three forced repairs use the same newcomer at ten

Needing all three removable corners means that each corner P separately occurs with S,T and four independent path sites: otherwise repairing the other two would suffice. Call a disjoint newcomer the helper of P. The six sites of P and its helper leave exactly four witness sites. Hence the other newcomer lies inside their union. If T helps P, then S minus T is contained in P; since S,T are distinct, P cannot also be disjoint from S.

If helpers vary along the connected three-corner piece, there are adjacent corners P,Q with opposite helpers. Say T helps P and S helps Q. Then T minus S is a nonempty subset of Q minus P. This last set is one site, because P,Q differ by one swap. Thus Q ⊆ P∪T. The four witness sites for P are therefore also blue to Q. Those four sites together with P,Q,T form a seven **before S is attached**, contrary to single-attachment legality. Hence all helpers coincide. If T is that helper, S minus T lies in the common intersection of C,D,E, namely {f}. Thus S is f plus two sites of T. No classification by labels is needed.

## Why a repairable failure before ten admits a single link

If a seven contains no parent, it cannot be repaired. If it contains one parent, that parent must be removable to repair it. Its support and its disjoint helper need six sites and the witness needs four more, impossible below ten. So in a repairable failure every seven has at least two parents. Both tips together cover U. A seven using them and no elbow needs three mutually blue sites outside U missed by both newcomers; but all three parents, S, and those three sites would form a seven before T is added. Thus every seven uses the elbow. If the elbow is removable, one link suffices.

If the elbow is not removable, two links could be necessary only if **both** elbow-tip pairs have their own witnesses, one excluding each tip. Otherwise one removable tip belongs to every seven. For each such pair the included tip must be removable. Its support and helper cover six sites; its witness needs three others. This already rules out width eight and below. At nine, the three sites of each witness are the entire complement of those six.

For the C,D witness this triple must contain Y, the only site added by the omitted E. Otherwise all three sites miss E too, contradicting single-attachment legality (three parents + S + three sites). Likewise the C,E witness must contain X. The remaining two sites in each triple are **the same set W**, namely all sites outside U∪S∪T: each witness exhausts the complement of its tip and helper, the only site in that complement hit by the omitted tip is Y (respectively X), and no witness site is hit by either newcomer. Consequently Y and X are each blue to both sites of W, which are blue to each other. X and Y are mutually blue because the missing fourth corner is a valid separated row. Now W∪{X,Y} is a four-site blue set missed by C,S,T. It completes a seven with **the elbow alone**. The elbow cannot be repaired, a contradiction. This proves the lower bound for two-link repairs, including witnesses not forced by single-parent obstructions.
